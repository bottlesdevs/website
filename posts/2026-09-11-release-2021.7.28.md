I am Mirko Brombin, the founder of Bottles and the only active maintainer of the Bottles Legacy project, I want to clarify that these words are mine
and are not necessarily shared by the contributors or the community.

For years we have been discussing Open Source, mainly talking about sustainability, maintainer burnout, contributions, governance, donations,
companies that use free software without giving anything back. These are real problems that need to be addressed, but there is an equally
serious and much less discussed problem.

What happens when a project slowly built by a community, perhaps for years, suddenly becomes interesting
to subjects that have far more funding, means and (sometimes) execution capabilities than the original maintainers?

Open Source code is, by definition, open, it can be studied, modified, redistributed and even forked. And that is right, it is the reason why
we chose and continue to choose Open Source every day instead of a proprietary path.

So what is the problem? Simply put, "the code is not the project", a project is also its history, the decisions made over time, the mistakes,
the trust built with users, the vision of those who created it (often) from nothing, the contributors, the reputation and above all the people who
kept it alive when almost nobody cared about it or when the project was going through its worst moments. None of this appears in a simple
`git clone`.

I want to be very clear, I do not believe forks are a problem, on the contrary an Open Source ecosystem in which forking were not possible would be profoundly less
free and would prevent what we have gained through all these years of experimentation. A fork can be born because the original project took a
wrong direction, because a community is not being listened to or because there is a need, precisely, to experiment without compromising the upstream vision, often also
because incompatible needs exist. All legitimate, nobody should be able to say "this project is mine, therefore you must accept any decision I make",
but there is an important difference between having the right to fork a project and considering the original project expendable (allow me the term).

Once, a fork often meant splitting a small community into two even smaller communities, on one hand improving things, on the other complicating them
for both. Today it can mean something completely different though, imagine a project maintained by a few people, developed for ten years, with
a stable community and a good product, then a new actor arrives with enormous funding, an already established platform and greater communication capabilities perhaps
with thousands of users immediately reachable. Technically the starting point is the same repository, practically however the two subjects are not competing on
equal terms. One built the project, the other can very quickly build a new identity around that same work. It can hire dedicated developers,
change the name (almost mandatory in practice), integrate the software into new environments, fund features, build a stronger narrative and become,
in the public perception, the new reference point.

All of this, while remaining compliant with the license, and this is precisely the point, Open Source licenses tell us what is legally permitted to do with the code, they do not
tell us what the best behavior is to keep the ecosystem healthy and they do not preserve the work done by maintainers who, today, remain the weak link.

The rule, for me, should be that if your goal is compatible with that of the upstream, first try to work with the upstream, instead of replacing it.
If a new scenario needs a new integration, talk about it with the maintainers, if the architecture needs to change, let us discuss it and if all of this is necessary in extremely
short timeframes.. let us discuss investments, it is drastically less costly if those who know the project and its past and present problems direct those funds.

No agreement can be found? The directions are opposite? The upstream is not interested? Or if there is a good answer to the question "Why am I forking instead of contributing
upstream?" then: Fork.

The other aspect that unfortunately in free software we tend to underestimate is the value of identity, a known name is not simply branding, it is an implicit pact
with users, when someone installs a project with a certain name, they expect certain people, processes, a certain philosophy, a relationship with the community.

In recent years I have started thinking about this more and more also by observing Bottles, behind what the user sees there are years of decisions, attempts, regressions,
components, dependencies, compatibility, infrastructure, user experience, third-party relationships and, above all, an enormous amount of community work. And attention, I do not think
this gives me or Bottles any special right over the problem of running Windows software on Linux, that would be absurd, anyone must be able to build alternatives, anyone
must be able to use similar ideas and anyone must be able to (within the limits of the license) fork the code, this is not the point. But I think we must stop behaving
as if the work accumulated by a community were merely an interchangeable technical base, behind a project there are people who at least deserve to be considered
before someone decides to rebuild elsewhere what they have maintained for years.

Open Source does not mean absence of responsibility or morality, on the contrary, the freedom granted by licenses makes the culture that
we build around that freedom even more important, we can choose to treat every repository as anonymous raw material, or, we can recognize that behind these repositories
there are histories, communities and people who have invested an enormous part of their lives in building them.

I honestly prefer the second option. This is why I believe that in the coming years we will need to talk much more about the preservation of Open Source projects. Not to prevent
forks, not to transform free software into proprietary software disguised as such.. but to prevent the freedom of the code from being interpreted as the irrelevance of
those who built it.

Perhaps the time has come for our ecosystem to start treating sustainability not only as a matter of funding, contributions and burnout, but also of respect,
because an Open Source project does not die only when its maintainers burn out or when resources are lacking, but also when someone decides that those who
built it were expendable.

Thank you for your attention, I hope these words help people think. There are no references to people, entities, groups, cases, nothing of the sort, only pure reflection.

_ Mirko B.
